Commercial water damage restoration is the work of removing standing water, finding hidden moisture, taking out materials that cannot dry and running drying equipment until readings are normal. We keep it to those jobs, and we explain the scope to you before any work starts.

If water is still flowing, the first call is to whoever controls the building's water and power. Then call us.

How water gets into a commercial space

Water in a larger building usually comes from a failed supply line, a sprinkler or water heater leak, a clogged drain, a roof opening or an upstairs unit. In a building with several rooms, water travels along ceilings, walls and floor seams well beyond the spot where it started.

That is why a quick mop-up rarely finishes the job. Moisture in the ceiling grid, wall base and floor layers keeps spreading until it is measured and dried.

A moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera confirm what the eye only suggests.

Safety first: the first hour in a wet building

  • Shut off the water at the main valve or the riser that feeds the wet area.
  • Turn off power to wet zones only if the panel is dry and you can reach it safely.
  • Keep staff and visitors out of standing water and away from sagging ceilings.
  • Call the property manager or owner so one person directs access.
  • Photograph the damage before anything is moved, then call the emergency line.

If water is near outlets, a panel or gas equipment, call the utility or 911 before anything else. A plumber has to stop a broken line, and a roofer has to close a roof opening. We handle the water that those trades leave behind.

Our process: extraction, moisture mapping and drying

Extract

Submersible pumps, extraction units and wet vacuums take the standing water off the floor.

Map

Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras mark wet walls, floors and ceilings on a simple sketch.

Remove

Wet materials that cannot dry, such as soaked ceiling tile or baseboard, come out.

Dry

Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run while we record readings in a drying log.

Moisture mapping matters most in larger buildings because the wet area is rarely the same as the visible stain. A drying log lets the manager or owner see readings fall day by day, so the equipment comes out when the numbers say it should.

Who decides what: owner, manager and tenant

Commercial jobs involve more than one party. We coordinate with the property manager or owner so one person approves the scope and controls access to shared areas.

Tenants and owners should each read their own lease and their own policy. The lease says who maintains the space and who pays for what, and the policy says what is covered. We can describe the work, but we cannot tell you whose responsibility it is.

Cost factors for a commercial water job

The price depends on the square footage that is wet, the water category, the materials involved, how many days of drying equipment the building needs and how much must be removed. Access matters too, such as work after hours to keep a space open.

We do not quote a number over the phone. The figure comes after the crew sees the space and measures it, and the scope is explained before work starts.

Insurance questions after a commercial loss

Texas Department of Insurance guidance for property owners is to notify your agent or company as soon as you know about damage. Some policies require notice of water damage sooner than others, so check yours today.

Sudden and accidental water is often treated differently from a slow leak, and flood is usually excluded. The TDI Consumer Help Line is 1-800-252-3439 if you have questions about your rights. Keep photos, drying logs and the written scope. We never promise approval.

Mistakes to avoid after a water loss

  • Waiting to see if wet ceiling tile dries by itself.
  • Running fans that push damp air into dry rooms.
  • Pulling out materials before photographing them.
  • Skipping the drying log and closing walls too soon.
  • Letting several people direct the work with no single contact.

Our crews work with reference to ANSI/IICRC S500, the published water damage standard, and you can ask any company whether its crews do the same.
