How to Fix Water Damaged Hardwood Floors in Duncanville, TX

Hardwood floors can often be saved if you stop the water and start drying within the first 48 hours. Here is a triage plan, plus how to read cupping and crowning and when water damage repair is the right call.

To fix water damaged hardwood, stop the water, remove standing water and rugs, get air moving, and run a dehumidifier. Do not sand or refinish until the wood is dry. Boards often flatten on their own as moisture leaves, and sanding too early can cause permanent damage. If boards are black, rotted or still wet after days, they usually need to be replaced.

The first 48 hours

Stop the water

Shut off the source and remove standing water with a wet vacuum.

Clear the floor

Lift rugs, furniture and wet items so air can reach the boards.

Move the air

Run fans and open windows if outdoor air is drier than indoor air.

Dehumidify

Run an LGR or household dehumidifier and watch the moisture readings.

Speed matters because wood absorbs water and swells. The longer it stays wet, the more it distorts and the more likely mold and finish damage become.

If the water came from sewage, a backed-up drain or outside flooding, keep people and pets away and call for help. Keep power off in wet areas unless you know it is safe and dry.

Take photos of the floor and note the time you found the water. Write down which rooms were wet. Those details help your insurer and help a crew judge how long the wood has been wet. Do not use fans to push dirty water around, and do not wait for the floor to dry on its own if the subfloor is soaked.

Reading cupping and crowning

Cupping means board edges rise higher than the center, usually because the bottom is wetter than the top. Crowning is the opposite, with the center higher than the edges, which can happen when the surface dries too fast or after sanding a cupped floor.

Both are signs of moisture imbalance. Do not sand to fix them until the wood is fully dry, or you will cut a crown into the floor.

Why the subfloor decides the outcome

The boards you see are only the top layer. Water runs under them and soaks the subfloor, and a floor can look fine while the layer beneath stays wet. That trapped water keeps pushing the boards upward and can feed mold.

A pin or pinless moisture meter reads both layers. Compare the reading on the boards to a dry area of the same wood, since a normal number varies by species and home. Drying mats and air movers pull water out from below when needed.

When boards can be saved

Floors dried within a couple of days from a clean water source have the best odds. Boards that are only slightly cupped often flatten after drying. A pro uses moisture meters, drying mats and air movers to pull water out of the boards and subfloor.

Wet cavities under the floor matter. If the subfloor is wet, water mitigation with drying equipment is the safer route than hoping it dries on its own.

Boards that are blackened, split, soft or still wet after several days usually come out. Sections can sometimes be replaced and blended in. Cost depends on the water category, area, materials, drying days and rebuild scope, and the real number comes from an on-site look.

What not to do while the floor is wet

  • Do not run a heater at full blast on the boards, since fast drying can split them.
  • Do not cover the floor with plastic or rugs, which traps moisture.
  • Do not walk on soaked boards in heavy shoes.
  • Do not use a steam cleaner or wet mop.
  • Do not refinish or seal until the readings are normal.

When sanding is safe

Sand only after moisture readings return to a normal level and the floor has been flat for a while. Sanding wet wood can lock in cupping and shrink gaps later. A flooring pro can tell you if the boards are thick enough to sand.

Industry practice follows ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it. We do not replace flooring installers, and we do not guarantee that a floor can be saved.
